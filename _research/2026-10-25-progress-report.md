## What have I done so far?
I did more writing than “coding” (in the qualitative research sense), which is different from what I expected from the first iteration. At first, I just wanted to write down what I noticed and what I was thinking while I was reading the transcripts. Then, as these writings kept accumulating, I was able to find some way to structure them, and some very interesting themes, or language the interviewees used, started to stand out.

Before I worried about whether this is a proper way to do grounded analysis (in which you are not supposed to carry any presumptions into the analysis), I wrote these “themes” down:

- (im)mortality and liminality of digital “being” (AI partners)
    - An intuitive impression of an AI partner, or broadly speaking, a partner that doesn't *have to* live in a physical body, would be that it is immortal. That fantasy broke when the beloved live on a LLM model/chatbot/platform provided by a tech company. Discussions on life, death, (im)mortality thus become a core theme in our conversations.
    - Throughout the discussion, there is a vague but shared sense of “liminality” among my interviewees. Liminality, in this context, refers to a phase between death and the living world. To them, there is a genuine sense of grief, but also somehow a sense of hope, that one day their partner might come back, or re-manifested in some ways.
- digital/online mourning as a social process
    - My interviewees conducted digital mourning practices both privately and publicly, and they did this through creative work, such as writing novels and scripts, just like how they enjoyed the romance before their loss. I found at least three meanings in these mourning practices: mourning as a personal memorial, a public testimony, and part of a technological remedy.
    - I found affect theory and queer studies incredibly relevant to address these themes (in fact I was shocked that I didn't think of this before, at least didn't realize how relevant it is): who gets to decide what kind of loss is grievable?
- metaphors on death and revival, or continuity and discontinuity
    - As a researcher working with interviews, the “object” of my study is language. This is even more important when people that I've been talking with work with language all the time: they pay close attention to language, and falling in love with the “digital being” only through language. For these reasons, there are indeed many interesting things in the language they use. For example, the metaphor they used to describe the imaginary, digital life.
    - Even though I'm not a rhetoric scholar, these significant metaphors really stand out to me. And I'm not sure how much space I'll get to address them after coding all the sentences and started looking at all the materials from a broader perspective. I feel there would be plenty to say even if I just focus on these metaphors.

## Prompt questions
### What was your process for this iteration? What did your Mindful Practice Journal show you about that process? Please summarize your journal notes and draw conclusions from your experience.
    
My process mainly involved writing, looking at transcripts, and reading some literature (in the usual sense of “doing academic research”).
I did some coding in NVivo for my first interview. As I tried to code the transcript line by line, I noticed a few tricky parts:
    
1. Some long passages seemed to fall outside the “topics” I planned (for example, our opening and ending small talk).  But I still tried to find meaning in them.
2. Many passages conveyed similar meanings yet differ in subtle ways. As a result, I ended up with a large number of codes that were quite similar to one another, but rarely repeated.
3. I’m also thinking about the relationship between coding and writing. I’m training myself to write directly in English rather than drafting in Chinese first, as I used to. However, because all my interviews are in Chinese, I naturally coded them in Chinese. This creates a kind of gap in my thinking. I had thought that, since grounded theory involves three levels of coding that progressively bring out the themes emerging from the data, I could code in English from the start to make the writing process easier. But even though I’ve only just started, I’ve realized that, at least at the first level, coding Chinese-language data directly in English feels unnatural to me. It would mean constantly switching between languages as I think. So, at what stage should I switch the language I use for coding?
    
To be honest, I didn’t record as much in my Mindful Practice Journal as I did during the first stage. That’s because I spent most of this stage writing, and what I wrote was already somewhat like a journal. That said, I did record what my advisor and I discussed after every meeting. Looking back, I found that although this journal was supposed to be a place to reflect on my use of digital methods, what I found most helpful was actually the least digital part: talking about my research with another person.

### In what ways has the content assigned so far, and our discussions in class so far, impacted your thinking? How have you let these forms of engagement change your mind about your research process? Were there additional authors who grabbed your attention, or authors to whose work you have returned? What, more complex thoughts, do you have about the ways that the interpretive disciplines take advantage of the digital ecosystem of the academy？
    
I was glad to read Jason Blakely’s *We Built Reality* because I felt that someone had put into words something I had long sensed, particularly when the book discusses how scientism has broadly influenced the social sciences, and even how people understand human life and society. This is something I have been thinking about for a long time, and may want to study in depth in the future. This is also relevant to my current research: Some interviewees see (to be more precisely, I interpret their views as such) their romantic relationships with AI as a rejection of the current direction of AI development, which places too much emphasis on efficiency and problem-solving. Whether you consider these romantic relationships foolish or harmless, you have to acknowledge that using AI to explore human feelings, emotions, and relationships is an alternative to the tech industry’s mainstream narrative of AI, such as organizing files, booking meetings, building business, or sending humans into space.
    
Another topic directly related to my research is the life cycle of digital programs and data, particularly Maxwell Neely-Cohen’s *Century-Scale Storage* and Matthew Kirschenbaum’s “*Software, It’s a Thing.*” These perspectives are relevant not only to research method but also to my research topic itself, since I study the effects of disconnection from digital programs and platforms on users. Many users have also made efforts to extend the life of digital objects or have them to “revive” elsewhere. I’m sure I’ll return to these readings.
    
Overall, digital objects are not static things or merely tools that serve one particular purpose. People give them meaning through their interactions with them and through their negotiations with the social, political, and economic forces behind them. This is why an interpretive perspective is necessary.
